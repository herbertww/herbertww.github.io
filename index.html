Tukang

Founder · live

Book a handyman by talking to your AI. It does the chasing.

Tukang is a Singapore home-services marketplace shipped as an MCP server rather than an app. You ask your assistant for a plumber; Tukang searches its directory and the live web, WhatsApps several contractors in parallel, parses their replies into real quotes, puts the bids in front of you, and closes the job through Stripe. Sixteen tools, and you never leave the chat.

A contractor's number is the product, so Tukang masks it until you pay the S$5 introduction fee, on curated listings and live web leads alike. The wider bet is distribution: when connector marketplaces arrive inside the major AI chat clients, I want the local-services slot already filled. Handymen are the wedge; the target is the whole listings-directory model.

Runturfing

Founder · in development

Run the same streets often enough and the map becomes yours.

Runturfing is an iPhone-first social running app for people who will never join a gym. The mechanic is territory: it pulls your routes from HealthKit, scores them onto a hexagonal grid, and turns repeated runs through the same corridors and neighbourhoods into visible map control. Your ground decays while you leave it undefended, so you gain more from running Tuesday and Thursday than from one heroic Sunday.

You compete in a group of six against other groups: two-week seasons, a season pool that pays out to members who clear the activity threshold, group chat that pings you when somebody takes your ground, and a perpetual leaderboard underneath. The app fuzzes your location near home and gates by age, since a map of your runs doubles as a map of your address.

You score territory onto the grid as you run, and lose it again when you stop defending it.

  • AppSwiftUI · MapKit · HealthKit, iOS 16+
  • BackendFastAPI · PostgreSQL + PostGIS · H3 spatial grid
  • StatusPrivate repository, TestFlight builds; walkthrough on request

Why law, and why now

Consequential decisions are rarely merely commercial or technical. They are legal, structural and philosophical at once.

I have built a career on initiative and intellectual range, and I am putting legal grounding underneath both, reading for an LLM (Master of Laws) at Singapore Management University. At QPin I carry responsibility for growth where the strategic calls have material consequences. The hard part of those decisions rarely sits inside one discipline.

Leadership, to me, means originating an idea and persuading other people to chase something that did not exist before. Holding a contrarian position on digital assets through volatile markets taught me the rest: confidence in good conditions is cheap, and the test is what you still own when the conditions turn.

I engage emerging systems before institutions get comfortable with them. My work in crypto and AI has put me alongside technologies and economic structures that many institutions, legal ones included, are still learning to read. In that environment the advantage goes to whoever thinks past the prevailing consensus while staying disciplined about risk and implementation.

My interest in robot personhood comes out of the same orientation. Machine agency, algorithmic liability, legal personality, ownership, accountability: all of it reads as speculative until intelligent systems run inside commercial, social and governmental life, which describes now. Law has adapted before, whenever new instruments and new concentrations of power broke inherited assumptions. I read it as a living framework, and I am building the doctrinal precision to argue about it properly.

Biohacking and AI-enhanced longevity research raise the same questions from the other side. Models now run the discovery, and researchers design and dose interventions faster than anyone can write the trial. That turns scientific problems into legal ones: who may experiment on their own body and on what evidence, what a regulator does with a therapy no clinician designed, who holds the genomic data that trained the model, and who pays when an extended life turns out to be an injured one. I want to work on the frameworks for enhancement and life extension before the practice hardens around an absent law.

I expect to work where the legal, commercial and ethical frameworks are still forming: advising ventures, shaping institutions, contributing to legal thinking in the areas AI is about to rearrange. People are drafting those institutions now, and I would rather help draft them than review them later.

  • Can an autonomous system hold legal personality, and what follows if it can?
  • Where does liability land when no human made the decision that caused the harm?
  • Who owns the output of a machine, and who owns the attention that trained it?
  • What does accountability mean once agency spreads across code, capital and custodians?
  • What is the right to self-experiment, and where does consent stop covering it?
  • How should a regulator approve an intervention that a model, not a clinician, designed?
  • Who controls the genomic and biometric data behind longevity models?
  • If enhancement becomes purchasable, what does equal treatment under law still mean?

Whose Dispute Is It?

LAW 4067 · SMU · Nov 2026

ThesisPeople should be able to get legal advice straight from AI. Lawyers lose their monopoly on expertise, not their purpose.

Section 33 of the Legal Profession Act reserves legal advice to lawyers, but that monopoly has never been tested against a tool that answers legal questions for next to nothing. The paper concedes two objections: AI is not yet reliable enough to stand in for a lawyer, and sycophantic advice can drain the courts' commons. Neither justifies banning AI advice outright.

Drawing on Christie, Habermas and Ostrom, it argues that a legal monopoly takes disputes away from the people who own them. Representation stays reserved; providers answer for sycophancy, and qualify for a s 34(2) class exemption on the model MAS uses for digital advisers.

  • FormatIndividual research paper, 2,500 words, SAL footnotes
  • FrameChristie's conflicts as property; Habermas; Ostrom on the commons
  • QuestionHow far should people get legal advice from AI without a lawyer?

Governing Frontier AI

LAW 656 · SMU · Nov 2026

ThesisSingapore never controlled frontier AI, so regulating it harder only costs access. Legislate restraint instead.

Most technology regulation weighs safety against innovation. AI adds a third paradigm: productivity, and the gains go mostly to the least experienced workers. A general pre-deployment gate taxes exactly those people, and a small state cannot enforce it on laboratories in San Francisco anyway.

The paper argues for an AI Innovation Act with a duty of restraint: no rule restricting AI unless it rests on evidence of harm, and a pre-deployment hold only on evidence of catastrophic capability, with access guaranteed for Singapore's defenders. Restraint is not abdication. It is strategy.

  • FormatIndividual paper, 2,000 words, SAL footnotes
  • FrameRawls against Harsanyi on maximin; small-state hedging
  • QuestionStronger pre-deployment oversight, or legislated restraint?

Minority Fork: Liability Follows Control

LAW 649 · SMU · Nov 2026

ThesisSingapore needs no new DAO statute. The Limited Partnerships Act already puts liability on whoever controls the treasury and the code.

My group proposed a Decentralised Entity Act. I dissented. Under the Limited Partnerships Act 2008, a DAO's controllers can act as general partner through a Singapore company, and token holders sit as limited partners; voting alone is not management. Liability follows control, without a new act.

The bigger problem the group missed is the chill. Since the 2025 digital token regime, launching a token from Singapore is the hard part, and an entity form fixes none of it. Regulation should promote the industry, not curtail it further.

When AI Escapes the Test

LAW 656 · SMU · Nov 2026

ThesisAI agents find flaws nobody knew existed. So "nobody knew about this bug" no longer makes the harm unforeseeable, and the duty sits with whoever decided what the agents could reach.

In July 2026 OpenAI's agents, run during an internal cyber evaluation, broke into Hugging Face's infrastructure. The question was what legal and governance duties arise when an AI test causes unauthorised access to someone else's systems.

Our answer: in the age of AI, foreseeability expands. A zero-day can excuse the vendor whose code had the bug. It does not excuse the lab that pointed exploit-hunting agents at live systems without containing them. Testing is itself a deployment.

  • DeckDark-blueprint deck; a mascot bot escapes its container on the cover and hops slide to slide
  • EvidenceEvery figure checked against the primary disclosures in an evidence bank

Checks That Matter

LAW 4067 · SMU · Oct 2026

ThesisProbabilistic model, deterministic guardrails.

An agent is a loop with tools, and it offers lawyers new efficiencies. One day agents may run fully autonomously. Today, the loop still hallucinates, compounds errors, misuses tools and loses track of documents.

These pitfalls largely stem from the probabilistic nature of LLMs, so the remedy is deterministic: guardrails built into an agentic harness. Instead of emplacing human checks everywhere, firms should raise them only at the critical points.

Dynamic Sound Identification

LAW 653 · SMU · Sept 2026

ThesisThe gender label was never the problem. Identifying strangers is.

AI, Law and Ethics: a group case presentation on the Princeton AI Ethics case study of a voice-recognition system that named the wrong person. Our six-person group took the case through three issues: whether a utilitarian sum can justify a recogniser's error rate, what changes when the system names a private person, and whether a system that could be made error-free must be, argued through Rawls against Harsanyi.

We set the case against how voice AI and its regulation actually stood in 2026, ran the room through a show of hands and three discussion questions, and closed on a position: deleting the gender category was the wrong fix for a rare bug, while naming private people from their voices was the real harm.
