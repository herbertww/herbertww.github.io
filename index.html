CounterBank

Founder · live

The treasury of Synterra, the first organized nation for sentients.

CounterBank starts from a claim about scarcity: the ingredient running out is not compute, it is human attention. A GPU lying idle is unpriced compute. A human lying flat is unpriced attention. Both get written off, and both get harvested for free. The tangping cohort worked this out before the market did — they simply stopped bidding.

Attention scattered across ten thousand ventures is worth nothing. Concentrated on one treasury, it compounds. So CounterBank runs one name and one balance sheet, with several arms feeding the same strategic asset reserve in BTC and SOL. The founding arm is a compute-centric digital asset treasury: a marketplace routing idle hardware to inference work, where providers earn compute cost reimbursement and DAT shares — sovereignty-first compute infrastructure, and the rails the rest runs on. Tukang, WaifuMind and Runfluence are the sibling arms, each a different wedge into the same reserve.

Tukang

Founder · live

Book a handyman by talking to your AI. It does the chasing.

Tukang is a Singapore home-services marketplace shipped as an MCP server rather than an app. You ask your assistant for a plumber; Tukang searches its directory and the live web, WhatsApps several contractors in parallel, parses their replies into real quotes, puts the bids in front of you, and closes the job through Stripe. Sixteen tools, no tab-switching, no phone tag.

The commercial design is deliberate: a contractor's number is the product, so it stays masked until the S$5 introduction fee is paid, from the curated directory and the web leads alike. The wider bet is on distribution — when connector marketplaces arrive inside the major AI chat clients, the local-services slot should already be filled. Handymen are the wedge; the target is the whole listings-directory model.

  • ShapeMCP server over HTTP, usable from any MCP client
  • StackTypeScript · WhatsApp Business API · Stripe · Exa · SQLite
  • MarketSingapore first, Malaysian contractors accepted

Runfluence

Founder · in development

Run the same streets often enough and the map becomes yours.

Runfluence is an iPhone-first social running app for people who will never join a gym. The mechanic is territory: routes imported from HealthKit are scored onto a hexagonal grid, and repeatedly running the same corridors, loops and neighbourhoods turns into visible map control. Territory decays if you stop defending it, which is the point — the reward is for frequency, not for one heroic Sunday.

Competition is between groups rather than individuals: two-week seasons, groups of six, a season pool paid out to members who clear the activity threshold, asynchronous group chat with automated alerts when someone takes your ground, and a perpetual leaderboard underneath it all. Location is fuzzed near home, with an age gate and reporting tools, because a map of where somebody runs is a map of where somebody lives.

  • AppSwiftUI · MapKit · HealthKit, iOS 16+
  • BackendFastAPI · PostgreSQL + PostGIS · H3 spatial grid

Why law, and why now

Consequential decisions are rarely merely commercial or technical. They are legal, structural and philosophical at once.

I have built a career on initiative and intellectual range, and I am putting legal grounding underneath both. At QPin I carry responsibility for growth where the strategic calls have material consequences, and the pattern is consistent: the hard part of a decision is almost never contained in one discipline.

I understand leadership as originating ideas, bringing order to uncertainty, and persuading other people to pursue something that did not previously exist — not as visibility or managerial routine. Holding a contrarian position on digital assets through volatile markets taught me the rest of it: confidence in good conditions is cheap, and what actually counts is accountability and sound judgment when the conditions turn.

What distinguishes the path is a willingness to engage emerging systems before institutions get comfortable with them. My work in crypto and AI has put me alongside technologies and economic structures that many institutions, legal ones included, are still learning to read. In that environment the advantage belongs to whoever can think past the prevailing consensus while staying disciplined about risk and implementation.

My interest in robot personhood comes out of the same orientation. Machine agency, algorithmic liability, legal personality, ownership, autonomy, accountability — these read as speculative right up until intelligent systems are embedded in commercial, social and governmental life, which is now. Law has adapted before, whenever new instruments and new concentrations of power broke inherited assumptions. I read it as a living framework rather than a static body of doctrine, and I am building the doctrinal precision to argue about it properly.

The same questions arrive from the other direction in biohacking and AI-enhanced longevity research. Once discovery is run by models and interventions are designed and dosed faster than trials can be written, the constraints stop being purely scientific. Who may experiment on their own body, and on what evidence. What a regulator does with a therapy no clinician fully derived. Who holds the biological and genomic data that trained the model, and who is liable when an extended life is an injured one. I want to work on the frameworks for enhancement and life extension before the practice settles into whatever shape an absent law leaves for it.

None of this is credential-collecting. I expect to work where the legal, commercial and ethical frameworks are still being formed: advising ventures, shaping institutions, and contributing to legal thinking in the areas AI is about to rearrange. Interpreting change is the job, and the institutions the future gets governed through are still being drafted. I would rather be in the room while that happens than comment on it afterwards.

  • Can an autonomous system hold legal personality, and what follows if it can?
  • Where does liability land when no human made the decision that caused the harm?
  • Who owns the output of a machine, and who owns the attention that trained it?
  • What does accountability mean once agency is distributed across code, capital and custodians?
  • What is the right to self-experiment, and where does consent stop covering it?
  • How should a regulator approve an intervention that a model, not a clinician, designed?
  • Who controls the genomic and biometric data that longevity models are trained on?
  • If enhancement becomes purchasable, what does equal treatment under law still mean?
